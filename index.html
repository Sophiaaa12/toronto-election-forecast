Toronto 2026 Municipal Election Forecast

An LLM silicon-sampling forecast with census post-stratification

Author

Jingwen Zhong, Siyi Zhu

Overview

Research question and objective

Every number on this page comes from synthetic voters: two large language models answering a ballot question while playing Census-shaped Toronto residents. No real person was surveyed.

Toronto elects a mayor and 25 ward councillors on Monday 26 October 2026, and almost no public polling exists for these races. Forecasting from a sample that does not represent the electorate is an old problem (Wang et al. 2015); here the sample does not contain voters at all. This project therefore asks:

Can a silicon sample — simulated voters answered by large language models — post-stratified to Census marginals, produce a transparent, reproducible forecast for all 26 Toronto municipal races, and what uncertainty and model disagreement does it imply?

The forecasting objective is a set of candidate-level vote-share distributions and conditional win frequencies for the mayoral race and all 25 ward councillor races, produced by a fully reproducible pipeline and communicated with honest uncertainty. Following the course framing, we are not evaluated on forecast accuracy itself (Gelman et al. 2020 on the goals of election forecasts), and nothing on this site should be read as a calibrated prediction of the real election.

The method is silicon sampling (Argyle et al. 2023), with the documented perils of LLM survey simulation kept front and centre (Bisbee et al. 2024; Heath and Alexander 2026).

Pipeline at a glance: LLM ballot responses (committed production sample: 3,042 synthetic personas × 26 races × 2 providers × 3 repeats = 36,504 requests, 0 technical failures) → census post-stratification (decided first-preference shares per race × provider × repeat) → Method C forecast draws (500 per race × provider: Dirichlet sampling uncertainty + centered multiplicative perturbation, target SD 4pp) → provider-specific forecasts, plus a descriptive equal-weight pooled summary.

Headline results:

  • The mayoral forecast collapses onto one name. In the frozen production run both models pick the incumbent, Olivia Chow, in 97.7% of valid mayoral responses, and her main challenger, Brad Bradford, in 0 of 16,406. When the daily tracker shows the same models that day’s headlines, the whole electorate swings to whoever the headlines favour (Bradford at 97–99% on 20 October, Chow at 99% on 21 October). The models answer from salience, not from the personas’ demographics; see the Discussion.
  • Ward races vary substantially, and the two providers name different leading candidates in 8 of 25 wards (leader defined by post-stratified point share; see Provider Comparison for the full reconciliation with the alternative definition).
  • Forecast uncertainty is assumption-driven: widening the assumed perturbation from 2pp to 6pp widens median leader intervals from 33.6pp to 41.0pp — an explicit, documented knob, not an empirical calibration.

ImportantWhat this forecast is — and is not
  • Is: a post-stratified summary of what LLMs say about a Census-shaped synthetic electorate, with model-based uncertainty from repeated sampling.
  • Is not: a poll, a survey, or a calibrated prediction of the real election. All win numbers are conditional simulation frequencies, not probabilities of winning the actual election. See Limitations.

The mayoral race

WarningMayoral mode collapse — read this before quoting any mayoral number

The mayoral forecast is not a competitive distribution. In the production silicon sample, Chow absorbs 97.7% of all valid mayoral choices (99.93% for Gemini, 95.00% for GPT); only 11 of the 53 mayoral candidates are ever chosen. The diagnostic experiment reproduced this under three ballot orders (see Diagnostics), so it is a property of the models rather than of ballot position, the same failure mode documented by Heath and Alexander (2026). No uncertainty method can honestly manufacture competitiveness from it, so the mayoral “forecast” should be read as a measured model output, not a horse race. The daily tracker shows that this output is not stable either: the same collapse moves to Brad Bradford when the day’s headlines favour him (see Daily Tracker and Discussion).

Method

  • Panel. A fixed panel of 1,200 personas: 4 drawn (seed 20261020) from each of the 300 ward × age × education census cells of the main forecast’s persona frame. Each panel persona carries its cell’s population share divided by 4, so ward and citywide weights match the full frame exactly (R/11_daily_panel.R). The same personas are asked every day, so changes come from the news context rather than from who is in the sample.
  • Questions. Each persona answers the mayoral race and their own ward’s councillor race, once per model per day: 1,200 × 2 races × 2 models = 4,800 requests a day.
  • Models. google/gemini-2.5-flash-lite and openai/gpt-4o-mini via OpenRouter, temperature 0.7. These are the same models and settings as the frozen production run.
  • News. Each morning the Action downloads the RSS feeds of CBC Toronto, the Toronto Star (GTA), CityNews Toronto, Global News Toronto, and the Toronto Sun (local news), and keeps up to 12 stories from the last 36 hours. Toronto election stories come first, then other election stories, then everything else, with at most 4 per outlet. Syndicated foreign wire copy is dropped. Headlines and RSS descriptions are used verbatim, with no LLM summary. If fewer than 2 feeds or 5 stories are available, that day is not polled. The day’s news file is committed to processed/daily/news/.
  • Prompt (v2). This is the production v1 prompt plus the date, the election date, and the day’s headlines (llm/prompts.py). As in production, the model only sees the persona’s ward, age group, and education group, and the candidate names for one race.
  • Estimates. Production post-stratification and Method C draws (target SD 4pp, 500 draws per race × model) are run on each day’s responses (R/12_daily_forecast.R). Headline figures are a 5-day trailing moving average of the cross-model mean, following the Synthetic Voter Project’s smoothing choice. Each day has a single repeat per persona and race, so there is more day-to-day noise than in the 3-repeat production run, especially in wards (about 48 panel personas per ward per model).
  • Data. The full history is in processed/daily/history.csv, one row per day × race × model × candidate. Raw responses are in processed/daily/responses/<date>/.

Reconciling two leader definitions

A “leading candidate” can be defined two ways, and they disagree in a few wards because the point estimates and the draws summarise different things (provider point estimates average three repeat cells; win frequencies average 500 draws that resample cells and add uncertainty layers):

Leader definition Wards where providers disagree
Highest post-stratified point share (used on this site) 8: W03, W04, W09, W13, W14, W20, W22, W23
Highest simulation win frequency 7: W03, W04, W14, W15, W20, W22, W23

The website uses the point-share definition throughout (Figure 2, Figure 5, Figure 6, Table 2, and this section). The three definitional edge cases are instructive:

  • Ward 09 — Gemini’s point shares are a near three-way tie (Martins, Marco 30.1%; Bravo, Alejandra 29.7%; one other 29.3%), so its top-point candidate is not its top-win-frequency candidate. GPT strongly favours the incumbent Bravo, Alejandra (82.5%, win frequency 100%), and by win frequency both providers name Bravo.
  • Ward 13 — GPT’s point shares are nearly tied (Harris, Morgan 24.7% vs incumbent Moise, Chris 24.0%), so GPT’s top-point candidate is Harris while its top-win-frequency candidate is Moise; Gemini strongly favours Moise (57.0%). By win frequency both providers name Moise.
  • Ward 15 — both providers’ top-point candidate is the incumbent Chernos Lin, Rachel (Gemini 48.0%, GPT 73.7%), but across Gemini’s three repeat cells the challenger Sharp, Sheena leads in the majority of simulation draws (win frequency 58.0% vs 41.8%), so the win-frequency definition flags a split that the point-share definition does not.

Two patterns matter regardless of definition:

  1. Provider disagreement is first-order. In several split wards one provider gives its leader a win frequency near 90% while the other gives its leader only ~35% — disagreement between models is far larger than the difference between any two uncertainty settings (Forecast Uncertainty).
  2. Providers answer differently. GPT-4o-mini declines (“undecided”) far more often, especially in the mayoral race (19.4% vs 0.8% — see Diagnostics), so the providers effectively estimate from different subsets of personas. We therefore report provider-specific forecasts throughout and never average them into a single “model of models”; the pooled column elsewhere is explicitly descriptive.

Forecast Uncertainty

Every number on this site comes from an explicit, layered simulation. The four sources of variation are kept distinct, because conflating them would overstate what the data can support. Shirani-Mehr et al. (2018) separate bias from variance in real election polls; our setting has no ground truth against which to make that separation.

  1. LLM response variability — each draw resamples one of the provider’s three independent repeat cells (the same persona-race asked three times at temperature 0.7).
  2. Dirichlet sampling uncertainty — shares ~ Dirichlet(\(n_{\text{eff}} \cdot \hat{s} + 0.5\)), where \(n_{\text{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i w_i^2\) is the Kish effective sample size of the post-stratified cell. This quantifies how much the estimate would move if the same personas were re-queried.
  3. Assumed forecast perturbation (Method C) — a centered multiplicative perturbation \(s_k \propto s_k \cdot e^{\sigma \varepsilon_k}\), \(\varepsilon \sim N(0,1)\), with \(\sigma\) calibrated per race so this layer alone gives the leader a standard deviation of the target SD (4pp default). It respects the probability simplex, adds no mass to zero-support candidates, and is only approximately center-preserving (realized maximum leader bias across all 52 race × provider units: 5.74pp, reported rather than assumed away).
  4. Real election uncertainty — not modelled. No layer accounts for turnout swings, campaign events, polling house effects, or systematic model error shared by both providers.
Note“Win frequency” ≠ “probability of winning”

Throughout this site, simulation win frequency means the fraction of 500 model-based draws in which a candidate has the highest simulated share, conditional on the LLM responses and the assumed perturbation scale. It is not a calibrated probability that the candidate wins the real 2026 election: we have no ground truth to calibrate against, and the source model itself may be systematically wrong. Communicating this gap honestly is the point of the exercise (Gelman et al. 2020; Kay et al. 2016). Currently 15 of 52 race × provider units show a win frequency of exactly 100%, and 18 of 52 sit at or below 70%.

Sensitivity to the assumed perturbation scale

How to read this figure. We re-ran the forecast draws three times, changing only one assumption: how much extra forecast uncertainty to add (the target SD, 2, 4 or 6 percentage points). Panel (a) tracks each race × provider leader’s 95% interval width across the three settings; every line slopes upward, so the intervals depend directly on this assumed knob. Panel (b) counts how many race × provider units still give their leader a 100% win frequency; that count shrinks as more uncertainty is assumed. Together they show that the width of our intervals and how many races look like a certain win are partly a modelling choice, not something the data pins down.

Because the perturbation scale is an assumption, not an estimate, we report a full sensitivity analysis at target SD ∈ {0.02, 0.04, 0.06} (common random numbers, so differences are purely the scale effect). Median leader interval width moves from 33.6pp → 37.3pp → 41.0pp, and the number of race × provider units with a degenerate 100% win frequency falls from 21 → 15 → 12. The published forecast uses 0.04; every alternative is committed in processed/forecast/sensitivity_target_sd.parquet.

Two further documented uncertainty facts: the Dirichlet +0.5 pseudo-count visibly shrinks leaders toward uniform in small-\(n_{\text{eff}}\), many-candidate wards (worst case Ward 19 / Gemini: 5.4% prior mass, ≈ −4.7pp on the leader — affects draws only; point estimates are unaffected), and Method C is only approximately center-preserving (max realized bias 5.74pp, worst: Ward 19 / Gemini).

Silicon Sampling Diagnostics

(a) Candidate-order invariance. In the diagnostic experiment each persona-race was asked under three ballot orders (the actual ballot order and two randomized orders). The mayoral choice was 100% order-invariant for both providers — confirming the collapse is knowledge-driven, not a ballot-position artifact. Ward-race choices were invariant in only 56–63% of complete valid triples (Gemini 56.3%, GPT 62.5%), i.e., ward preferences are meaningfully order-sensitive, a property of LLM survey responses documented by Murray and Alexander (2026). We retain it rather than suppress it by randomizing ballot order in production (seed 20261026).

(b) Mayoral response concentration. Chow takes 99.9% (Gemini) and 95.0% (GPT) of valid mayoral choices; the remaining mass is spread over ~10 minor candidates.

(c) Provider undecided rates. GPT-4o-mini answers “undecided” on 19.4% of mayoral and 3.9% of ward queries; Gemini on 0.8% and 1.0%. Undecided responses are excluded from estimation by design (the estimand is the decided-voter share), which means the providers effectively condition on different responder pools — the main reason we keep forecasts separate.

Full diagnostic design and verdicts: docs/llm_diagnostic_experiment.md and docs/llm_diagnostic_report.md (committed).

The Census does not publish an age × education joint distribution, so within each ward we approximate \(P(\text{Age}, \text{Education} \mid \text{Ward}) \approx P(\text{Age} \mid \text{Ward})\,P(\text{Education} \mid \text{Ward})\) — an independence approximation documented in the README and carried as a limitation. This yields 12 cells per ward and 300 population cells citywide (4 age groups × 3 education groups × 25 wards, population 20+).

Silicon sampling

  • Personas. 3,042 synthetic personas allocated to the 300 cells proportionally to each cell’s share of the citywide 20+ population, with a floor of 5 per cell. Each persona carries its Census design weight (below).
  • Providers and repetition. Two models through OpenRouter — google/gemini-2.5-flash-lite and openai/gpt-4o-mini — each queried three independent times per persona-race pair at temperature 0.7, max 256 tokens, prompt version v1: 3,042 × 26 × 2 × 3 = 36,504 requests.
  • Prompt construction. Each request gives the model only the persona’s ward, age group, and education group, plus that race’s candidate list (ID + ballot name). It never sees weights, other personas, biographies, policy positions, or historical results. Candidate order is randomized per request (seed 20261026) because LLM choices are order-sensitive (see Diagnostics; Murray and Alexander (2026)).
  • Validation and reliability. Responses are strictly validated JSON; invalid replies are re-sampled (13 in total); technical failures were retried with backoff and the run finished with 0 technical failures. Responses are disk-cached (SHA-256 over the full request specification), every request is cost-logged, and a hard budget of $3.75 stopped the run if needed (it was not).
  • Timing. The pilot and ballot-order experiments ran on the evening of 17 October 2026 (Toronto time). The production run started at 13:35 on 18 October and finished at 23:13 the same night, roughly ten hours of wall-clock time including one interruption and resume; the median request took under one second.
  • Frozen artefact. The production response file processed/production/llm_responses.parquet (36,504 rows) is frozen with md5 420c3051da6c9a291f5fcf7b587e06bf; the forecast pipeline halts if it ever changes.

Post-stratification

For each race × provider × repeat, over valid (decided) responses only, each persona \(i\) carries its design weight \(w_i\) (cell population share divided by personas in the cell; weights sum to 1 citywide):

\[\hat{s}_k \;=\; \frac{\sum_i w_i \,\mathbf{1}[\text{choice}_i = k]}{\sum_i w_i}\]

Undecided and not-voting replies are excluded from numerator and denominator: the estimand is each candidate’s share of decided first preferences, not turnout. Ward races sum over that ward’s personas; the mayoral race sums over all personas with citywide weights, so wards enter in proportion to their 20+ population.

Provider point estimates average the provider’s three repeat cells equally. A separately labelled descriptive pooled estimate averages all six cells equally; it is a summary for readers, never used as “the” forecast.

Software

The language models are GPT-4o mini (OpenAI 2024) and Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite (Google DeepMind 2025), both reached through OpenRouter (OpenRouter 2026). The query pipeline is written in Python (Python Software Foundation 2025) with requests (Reitz 2025) and PyArrow (Apache Arrow Developers 2026), and tested with pytest (Krekel et al. 2026). Cleaning, post-stratification, simulation, and figures use R (R Core Team 2024) with dplyr (Wickham et al. 2023), tidyr (Wickham, Vaughan, et al. 2024), readr (Wickham, Hester, et al. 2024), readxl (Wickham and Bryan 2023), jsonlite (Ooms 2014), digest (Eddelbuettel 2024), nanoparquet (Csárdi and Mühleisen 2025), sf (Pebesma and Bivand 2023), ggplot2 (Wickham 2016), scales (Wickham et al. 2025), patchwork (Pedersen 2024), ggrepel (Slowikowski 2026), ragg (Pedersen and Shemanarev 2026), and systemfonts (Pedersen et al. 2024); tests use testthat (Wickham 2011) and code is formatted with styler (Müller et al. 2025). The site is built with Quarto (Allaire et al. 2026) and set in IBM Plex Sans (Abbink and Bold Monday 2018).

Discussion

What a silicon sample measures in a local election

Argyle et al. (2023) showed that a language model conditioned on detailed American demographic and partisan backstories could reproduce the vote-choice patterns of the groups those backstories describe, which they called algorithmic fidelity. That result depended on two things this election lacks: party labels that the model can map from demographics to a vote, and a large volume of text linking the two. Toronto’s municipal elections have no party labels, and our personas carry only a ward, an age band, and an education band. With so little to condition on, the model falls back on what it knows about the candidates’ names. In the frozen run that knowledge is almost entirely about one name: Olivia Chow receives 97.7% of valid mayoral choices and Brad Bradford, whom local coverage describes as her main challenger, receives none of 16,406.

The collapse follows salience, not demographics

Bisbee et al. (2024) found that synthetic survey answers have far less variance than real ones and shift with prompt wording and with the date of the query. Our daily tracker isolates the second effect. Its 1,200 personas, models, and settings are fixed from day to day; only the headlines change. On 20 October, with headlines reporting Bradford pulling ahead, both models gave Bradford 97–99% of decided votes. On 21 October, with headlines about criticism of his water plan, both gave Chow about 99%. A demographic group did not move; the whole synthetic electorate did, at once and in the same direction. This is the mode collapse that Heath and Alexander (2026) document, but it is not fixed on the incumbent: it attaches to whichever candidate the context makes salient. It also fits Santurkar et al. (2023), who found that steering a model toward a demographic group changes its opinion distribution much less than the real gap between groups, and Wang et al. (2024), who show that models flatten the variation within identity groups. In our data the persona’s age and education barely matter to the mayoral answer.

Analyst choices are first-order

Murray and Alexander (2026) show that apparently minor analyst choices change silicon samples substantially. We see the same at every stage we varied. Changing the candidate order changed the ward-race answer for 37–44% of persona-race pairs. Changing the provider changed the leading candidate in 8 of 25 wards. Changing the assumed forecast-noise SD from 2 to 6 points widened the median leader interval by 7 points. Adding a day’s headlines replaced the mayoral leader outright. Each of these is larger than the sampling uncertainty that the Dirichlet layer reports, which is why we publish both providers separately and show every sensitivity rather than one blended number.

What post-stratification can and cannot fix

Wang et al. (2015) forecast a US election accurately from a heavily skewed sample of Xbox users, because multilevel regression and post-stratification can reweight a sample that is unrepresentative but still contains real differences between groups. Post-stratification cannot create differences that are not there. When every Census cell gives the same answer, as in the mayoral race, the weights change nothing, and the effective sample is closer to one respondent than to 3,042. Ward races vary more across cells and across providers, so their post-stratified shares carry more information, but that information is about the models. Our conclusion is that, for a low-information local election, a silicon sample is better read as a measurement of what two models find salient than as a survey of voters. Its most useful outputs are diagnostic: where providers disagree, how much the answer depends on ballot order, and how quickly it moves with the news. That is the statement about information sources that Gelman et al. (2020) argue a forecast should make explicit.

Limitations

  1. No external calibration. Toronto 2026 has no published polling and we do not calibrate against historical election errors, so simulation win frequencies must not be quoted as real winning probabilities (Gelman et al. 2020).
  2. Mayoral mode collapse — 97.7% single-candidate concentration, invariant to ballot order — is a property of the models’ world knowledge; the mayoral output is effectively single-candidate (Heath and Alexander 2026).
  3. No turnout model. Only 2 of 36,504 replies were “not voting”; turnout intent is not simulated, so results are decided-preference shares, not forecast valid-vote shares.
  4. Informative undecided responses. GPT-4o-mini declines 19.4% of mayoral queries; exclusion without nonresponse adjustment conditions each provider on a different responder pool, and the exclusion mechanism itself may correlate with candidate preference (Bisbee et al. 2024).
  5. Census independence approximation. Age × education cells within wards are built from marginal products, which can misallocate weight when the traits are correlated.
  6. Provider disagreement dominates method choices in 8 of 25 wards — a single pooled forecast would hide genuine model uncertainty.
  7. Small effective samples (\(n_{\text{eff}}\) ≈ 79–165 in wards) give wide intervals; the Dirichlet +0.5 prior adds up to ≈ −4.7pp of leader shrinkage in the worst ward (draws only), and Method C is only approximately center-preserving (max realized bias 5.74pp) with an assumed, sensitivity-tested perturbation scale.
  8. Simulated voters are not voters. The entire electorate is synthetic; the forecast measures what two LLMs say about a Census-shaped persona frame (Bisbee et al. 2024).

Conclusion and Future Work

Silicon sampling with census post-stratification can produce a complete, reproducible, openly documented forecast for all 26 Toronto municipal races from a $3.16 production run. What it produces is a measurement of two language models. Without news, the mayoral race is not a race in the models’ world (Chow at 97.7% of valid responses, unchanged by ballot order); with a day’s headlines, the entire synthetic electorate moves to whichever candidate the headlines favour. Ward races are more varied, and the two providers disagree about the leader in 8 of 25 wards, a model-uncertainty signal we report rather than average away. The uncertainty we report is explicitly layered and its assumptions (target SD) are sensitivity-tested, but it remains conditional on model outputs and uncalibrated to real election error.

Natural next steps, in order of value:

  1. Calibrate after 26 October. Score every provider forecast against the official results with Brier and log scores, and measure the real coverage of the simulation intervals — turning the current honest disclaimer into an empirical error estimate.
  2. Model the undecided. Treat “undecided” as an outcome to be modelled (e.g., a separate post-stratified rate with principled allocation) instead of dropping it.
  3. Correct mode collapse following Heath and Alexander (2026) (semantic similarity rating) and re-test whether the mayoral race becomes informative.
  4. Strengthen the frame by obtaining age × education joint counts (e.g., PUMF) to remove the independence approximation, and adding a turnout layer informed by City of Toronto (2022b).
  5. Combine providers with an explicit hierarchical model that treats provider as a random effect, instead of reporting arms separately.

# 1. Data (official City of Toronto downloads, checksum-verified)
Rscript download_data.R && Rscript prepare_data.R

# 2. Population frame and synthetic personas (seeded)
Rscript R/run_pipeline.R

# 3. Silicon sampling (Python; requires OPENROUTER_API_KEY — NOT needed to

Rscript tests/run_tests.R
python3 -m pytest tests/python -q

# 6. Website figures, tables, and site
Rscript website/make_figures.R
quarto render website
